01 · Human Eye Science

Learn how the eye receives light and how the visual system turns it into meaningful information.

Lesson 1 — The eye as an optical system

The human eye is a biological optical system. It collects light from the environment, bends and focuses that light, detects it with specialised cells, and sends electrical signals to the brain. The brain interprets those signals as the visual experience we recognise.

Optical components

Cornea, aqueous humour, pupil, crystalline lens and vitreous humour guide and focus incoming light.

Neural components

Retina, optic nerve and visual pathways carry and process information from the eye.

Journey of light through the eye

  1. Cornea: the transparent front surface provides much of the eye's initial refractive power.
  2. Aqueous humour and pupil: light passes through the fluid-filled front chamber and the pupil opening.
  3. Crystalline lens: fine-tunes focus, changing shape during accommodation.
  4. Vitreous humour: light travels through the transparent gel filling the large rear chamber.
  5. Retina: photoreceptors absorb light and initiate neural signals.
  6. Optic nerve: signals travel toward the brain for further processing.
Simplified educational cross-section; anatomical proportions are not to scale.

Lesson 2 — Retina: rods and cones

The retina is a light-sensitive neural tissue lining the back of the eye. It contains two major classes of photoreceptors:

Rods

Very sensitive in dim light. Important for night and peripheral vision. They do not provide colour discrimination like cones.

Cones

Work best in brighter conditions. Support colour vision and fine detail. Cone density is highest near the fovea.

Why is the fovea important?

The fovea is a small central retinal region specialised for high-acuity vision. When you look directly at a small detail, your eyes position its image near this region. Peripheral retina is more useful for broad awareness and motion detection.

Key idea: High visual acuity depends on more than simply having a retina. It also depends on optics, photoreceptor arrangement, neural processing, lighting and the task being performed.

Lesson 3 — Accommodation and focus

When viewing a near object, the ciliary muscle contracts, zonular tension reduces and the elastic lens becomes rounder. This increases the lens's optical power. For distant viewing, the lens is flatter and its power is lower.

Viewing targetLens shapeOptical effect
DistantFlatterLower lens power
NearRounder / thickerHigher lens power

Common refractive conditions

Myopia: distant objects appear blurred because the eye's optical system focuses the image in front of the retina under relaxed viewing.

Hyperopia: the optical system would focus light behind the retina if accommodation did not compensate.

Astigmatism: different meridians have different refractive powers, so light is not focused to one single point.

Presbyopia: age-related reduction in the ability to accommodate for near tasks.

02 · Optotype Laboratory

Understand visual acuity, standardised symbols and the mathematics behind an acuity chart.

Lesson 1 — What does visual acuity mean?

Visual acuity describes the ability to resolve fine detail under specified test conditions. It is commonly measured using standardised optotypes at a defined distance. Acuity is not the same as the entire quality of vision: contrast sensitivity, visual field, colour perception and depth perception are separate functions.

Optotype

A standardised letter or symbol designed for acuity testing, with controlled overall size and critical detail.

Test distance

The distance between the eye and the chart. The physical size of a symbol must be appropriate for this distance.

Examples of optotype families

  • Snellen letters: familiar letters arranged in rows of decreasing size.
  • Tumbling E: a block E pointing in different directions; the person indicates the direction of the open side.
  • Landolt C: a ring with a gap; the person identifies the gap direction.
  • logMAR / ETDRS: structured chart systems designed for more consistent measurement, with controlled progression and spacing.

Lesson 2 — Snellen fraction explained

In a Snellen fraction such as 20/40, the numerator is the test distance in feet and the denominator represents the distance at which a person with the reference acuity would be expected to read that line. In metric notation, 6/12 is the corresponding form.

Snellen fraction = Test distance / Reference distance

For example, 6/12 means the person is tested at 6 metres while the line is designed to be read by a reference observer at 12 metres. It is a notation for acuity performance under the test conditions, not a percentage score.

Important: Snellen fractions are not a complete description of visual function. Clinical interpretation depends on the chart, protocol, correction status, distance and examination conditions.

Lesson 3 — Angular size and the 5-minute rule

A standard 20/20 Snellen optotype is designed so its full height subtends approximately 5 arcminutes at the specified distance. The critical stroke or gap detail subtends approximately 1 arcminute.

Visual angle links physical object size to viewing distance. A small object close to the eye can subtend the same angle as a larger object farther away.

H = 2 × D × tan(θ / 2)

Where H is object height, D is viewing distance and θ is visual angle. Use consistent units for H and D.

03 · Digital Imaging Science

Connect the language of camera technology with pixels, sampling and screen displays.

Lesson 1 — What is a pixel?

A digital image is represented as a grid of picture elements called pixels. Each pixel stores numerical information describing colour and brightness. A simple RGB image commonly represents each pixel using red, green and blue channel values.

Resolution

The number of samples across an image, often written as width × height, such as 4000 × 3000.

Pixel dimensions

The width and height of the image in pixels. These dimensions determine the total pixel count.

Explore a pixel grid

Increase the number of cells. Notice that a higher sampling grid can represent finer spatial changes, although the real result also depends on optics, focus, sensor quality and processing.

Lesson 2 — Megapixels and image dimensions

One megapixel is one million pixels. Multiply image width by image height to calculate total pixels, then divide by one million to express the result in megapixels.

Megapixels = (Width × Height) ÷ 1,000,000

Does more megapixels always mean a better photo?

No. Pixel count is only one factor. Lens sharpness, sensor area, pixel design, focus accuracy, exposure, noise, dynamic range, stabilisation and image processing all contribute to the final image.

Lesson 3 — Image sensors and Bayer filters

Many consumer cameras use a colour filter array over a sensor. A common arrangement is the Bayer pattern, which uses twice as many green-filtered photosites as red or blue, often in a repeating 2 × 2 pattern.

Each photosite measures light through its own colour filter; it does not independently capture a full RGB triplet.

A processing step called demosaicing estimates missing colour-channel values using neighbouring photosites to construct a full-colour image. This is one reason sensor output is processed before a finished photograph is displayed.

Lesson 4 — PPI, DPI and display size

PPI means pixels per inch and describes pixel density on a digital display or in a digital image layout. DPI means dots per inch and is commonly used for printer output. They are related concepts but not interchangeable.

For a display, physical size matters. A chart drawn as 200 CSS pixels does not necessarily measure 200 physical screen pixels, and operating-system scaling or browser zoom can change the relationship. Clinical displays require calibration against a physical measurement.

Camera connection

A camera sensor samples an optical image. A human eye also has spatial sampling limits, but camera pixels and human visual acuity are different measurement systems and cannot be directly converted.

04 · Optics & Physics

Understand how light bends, how lenses focus and how visual angle connects size with distance.

Lesson 1 — Light and refraction

Light changes direction when it passes obliquely from one transparent medium into another with a different refractive index. This phenomenon is called refraction. The speed of light differs in different media, and the direction change is described by Snell's law.

n₁ sin(θ₁) = n₂ sin(θ₂)

Here n₁ and n₂ are refractive indices, while θ₁ and θ₂ are angles measured from the normal to the boundary. When light enters a medium with a higher refractive index, it bends toward the normal.

Conceptual ray diagram; not drawn to scale.

Lesson 2 — Convex and concave lenses

Convex lens

Thicker at the centre than at the edges. A converging lens brings parallel incoming rays toward a focal point.

Concave lens

Thinner at the centre than at the edges. A diverging lens spreads parallel incoming rays outward.

Focal length

Focal length is the distance between a lens's principal plane and its focal point under the paraxial approximation. It is commonly measured in metres or millimetres. A shorter focal length corresponds to greater optical power for a thin lens in air.

Lesson 3 — Lens power and diopters

Optical power is the reciprocal of focal length when focal length is expressed in metres.

P (diopters) = 1 ÷ f (metres)

A lens with focal length 0.50 m has power +2.00 D if converging. A diverging lens is conventionally assigned negative power.

Lesson 4 — Visual angle

The visual angle is the angle an object subtends at the eye. It depends on the object's physical size and the viewing distance. This concept is important in optotype design, display design and visual ergonomics.

θ = 2 × arctan(H / (2D))

For small angles, θ in radians is approximately H ÷ D. Always use consistent units.

Practical link: If viewing distance doubles while object height stays constant, the visual angle becomes smaller. The object appears smaller in angular terms.

Units reminder

1 metre = 100 centimetres = 1000 millimetres. Convert focal length to metres before calculating diopters.

05 · Human Visual Perception

Explore how the visual system interprets contrast, colour, depth, movement and space.

Lesson 1 — Seeing is more than detecting light

The eyes collect visual information, but perception is an active process involving the brain. The brain combines incoming signals with context, attention, experience and information from other senses. This is why two people may interpret an ambiguous image differently, or why an optical illusion can appear convincing even when we know how it works.

Lesson 2 — Contrast sensitivity

Contrast describes the difference in luminance between an object and its background. A high-contrast black letter on a white background is usually easier to see than a low-contrast grey letter on a similar grey background.

High contrast

Large luminance difference. Fine details are generally easier to distinguish.

A B C
Low contrast

Small luminance difference. Detail may become harder to detect, even if the symbol size is unchanged.

A B C

Contrast sensitivity is not identical to visual acuity. A person may read large high-contrast letters but still have difficulty detecting objects in low-contrast conditions.

Lesson 3 — Colour vision

Human colour vision in typical photopic conditions depends on three cone classes with different spectral sensitivities, often described as S, M and L cones. The brain compares their responses to construct colour experiences. Colour perception also depends on lighting and surrounding context.

Colour vision differences can affect how certain colour combinations are distinguished. Good interface design should not use colour as the only way to communicate important information; combine colour with labels, shapes or patterns.

Lesson 4 — Depth perception and visual field

Depth perception uses several cues. Binocular disparity compares the slightly different images from the two eyes. Monocular cues include relative size, overlap, perspective, shading and motion parallax.

Visual field is the area that can be seen while gaze is directed at a fixed point. Central vision supports detailed tasks; peripheral vision contributes to awareness of the surrounding environment.

Design implication: A vision-related interface should use readable typography, adequate contrast, clear focus states, predictable navigation and accessible alternatives to colour-only cues.

06 · Healthcare Technology

Translate scientific understanding into responsible digital tools and reliable testing workflows.

Lesson 1 — Anatomy of a digital vision system

A digital vision-testing product is not simply a chart displayed on a screen. It is a system made up of hardware, software, protocols, people, environment and quality controls.

Presentation layer

Displays validated optotypes, instructions and examiner controls.

Calibration layer

Accounts for display dimensions, pixel density, scaling and viewing distance.

Test engine

Controls test sequence, eye selection, response capture and scoring rules.

Records and reporting

Stores results, test conditions, examiner details and report history.

Lesson 2 — Why calibration matters

A chart must have known physical dimensions if it is intended to represent a specified visual angle. A browser's CSS pixel is a logical unit, not a guaranteed physical millimetre on the monitor. Display scaling, browser zoom, device pixel ratio and monitor settings can all change the relationship.

Example calibration workflow

  1. Identify the display and its native resolution.
  2. Measure the active physical screen width and height.
  3. Determine the effective pixel density and account for operating-system scaling.
  4. Set the intended viewing distance.
  5. Render a calibration object and verify it using a physical ruler.
  6. Lock or monitor browser zoom and display settings during the test.
  7. Record calibration details and re-check when the setup changes.
Clinical caution: A successful ruler check alone does not establish clinical validity. A clinical product requires validated protocols, verification, risk management, usability testing and appropriate regulatory assessment.

Lesson 4 — Build responsibly

For a real clinical product, consider software lifecycle controls, risk management, usability engineering, quality management, cybersecurity, privacy, verification and clinical validation. In India, medical-device software obligations depend on intended purpose and regulatory classification. Seek qualified regulatory advice before clinical deployment or marketing claims.

Useful standards to investigate with specialists include IEC 62304 (medical-device software lifecycle), ISO 14971 (risk management), IEC 62366-1 (usability engineering) and ISO 13485 (quality management systems).
